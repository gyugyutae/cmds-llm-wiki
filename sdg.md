## 부연설명

### 1. SDGs의 세 가지 축

SDGs는 **사회(Social), 경제(Economic), 환경(Environmental)** 문제를 함께 해결하려는 목표이다. 세 영역은 서로 연결되어 있다. 예를 들어 기후변화로 농업이 피해를 입으면 일자리와 소득이 줄어들고, 이것이 다시 빈곤과 교육 기회의 부족으로 이어질 수 있다.

### 2. The rule of law

`the rule of law`는 권력자를 포함한 모든 사람이 법의 적용을 받는다는 의미이다. `Law is number one`보다 아래 문장이 더 자연스럽다.

> No one is above the law.

법치주의, 정의, 평화, 책임 있는 제도는 **SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions**와 연결된다.

트럼프 취임식에 기업인들이 참석한 사례는 정치와 기업 권력의 관계를 토론할 때 사용할 수 있다. 다만 참석 자체만으로 법치주의가 약해졌다고 단정하기는 어려우므로, 수업에서는 `Some people argue that...`처럼 조심스럽게 말하면 좋다.

### 3. Neocolonialism

`neocolonialism`은 식민 지배가 공식적으로 끝난 뒤에도 강한 국가가 경제적·정치적 영향력을 통해 다른 국가에 계속 영향을 미치는 현상을 뜻한다. 빈곤 문제에서는 Global North와 Global South 사이의 자원과 부의 불평등을 설명할 때 사용할 수 있다.

### 4. 빈곤의 원인

- **Social:** 교육, 기술, 복지에 접근하기 어려우면 가난에서 벗어날 기회가 줄어든다.
- **Economic:** 소득과 자원이 불평등하게 분배되면 일부 지역과 집단에 빈곤이 계속될 수 있다.
- **Environmental:** 기후변화는 농업, 식량, 물, 주거 환경에 피해를 주며 가난한 지역에 더 큰 부담을 줄 수 있다.

영어로 답할 때는 다음 순서가 편하다.

> Poverty has social, economic, and environmental causes.  
> First, ... Second, ... Finally, ...

### 5. 빈곤의 영향

- **Individual:** 건강과 생명이 위험해지고 교육 기회와 인간다운 생활을 잃을 수 있다.
- **National:** 경제 성장과 공공서비스에 부담을 주고 사회 불안을 키울 수 있다.
- **Global:** 난민, 강제이주, 감염병과 같은 국제적인 문제로 이어질 수 있다.

범죄율과 빈곤은 관계가 복잡하기 때문에 `Poverty causes crime`이라고 단정하기보다 아래처럼 말하는 것이 좋다.

> Poverty can contribute to higher crime rates.

### 6. Maslow's hierarchy of needs와 연결

빈곤한 사람은 음식, 물, 집, 안전과 같은 기본적인 욕구를 먼저 해결해야 한다. 따라서 교육이나 장기적인 목표에 집중하기 어려울 수 있다.

**Live Podcast: Why We Fight**

**Topic: The Military-Industrial Complex**

  

**Characters**

  

- **Host (H):** Podcast host and moderator
- **Expert (E):** Expert on international politics and the military-industrial complex
- **Citizen 1 (C1):** Ordinary citizen with a family connection to war
- **Citizen 2 (C2):** Young citizen who shares a personal perspective on military service and economic opportunity

  

⸻

  

**1. Documentary Overview — About 2 minutes**

  

**H:**

Hello everyone, and welcome to our podcast, Today, we’re going to talk about the documentary _Why We Fight_, directed by Eugene Jarecki in 2005.

  

The documentary asks a simple but powerful question: **Why does America fight so many wars?**

  

At first, we might think the answer is national security, protecting democracy, or defending freedom. But the documentary asks us to look deeper.

  

**E:**

Exactly. The main issue of the documentary is the **military-industrial complex**.

  

The term became famous when President Dwight D. Eisenhower used it in his farewell address in 1961. Eisenhower warned that the growing relationship between the military, the defense industry, and the government could become too powerful.

  

The documentary looks at how these relationships developed after World War II and how they influenced American foreign policy, especially the Iraq War.

  

**H:**

So, the documentary isn’t simply asking whether war is necessary.

  

It’s asking another question:

  

**“Who benefits from war, and how can war influence political decisions?”**

  

**C1:**

And I think that’s what makes the documentary interesting. It doesn’t only talk about presidents or military leaders. It also shows ordinary people whose lives are directly affected by war.

  

**C2:**

Right. So today, we’re going to look at the military-industrial complex from different perspectives: political, economic, and personal.

  

⸻

  

**2. What Is the Military-Industrial Complex?**

  

**H:**

Let’s start with the basic question.

  

For someone who has never heard the term before, what exactly is a **military-industrial complex**?

  

Could you elaborate on that?

  

**E:**

Simply put, it describes the close relationship between the **military, the government, defense companies, and political institutions**.

  

  

The problem isn’t that defense companies exist. Every country needs some level of national defense.

  

The concern is that when these interests become too closely connected, military spending can become politically and economically difficult to reduce.

  

**H:**

So you’re saying that the issue isn’t simply “weapons companies make money.”

  

It’s that economic interests can potentially influence political decisions about war and military spending.

  

**E:**

Exactly.

  

And the documentary gives us an interesting example.

  

It explains how military projects can be spread across different states and communities. When a weapons program creates jobs in many areas, members of Congress may have a strong reason to protect that program.

  

So even if someone wants to reduce military spending, they may face pressure from their own voters and local industries.

  

**C2:**

That actually makes the issue more complicated than I expected.

  

Because from the perspective of a local community, a defense company isn’t just a weapons company.

  

It’s someone’s job.

  

**E:**

That’s an important point.

  

The military-industrial complex doesn’t survive only because of powerful politicians or CEOs.

  

It can also become part of the everyday economy.

  

⸻

  

**3. But Does That Mean War Is About Money?**

  

**H:**

I see your point, but are we saying that America goes to war simply because companies want to make money? How would you respond to critics who argue that war is far more complex than just profit?

  

Because that sounds like a very simple explanation for something as complicated as war.

  

**E:**

No, I wouldn’t say that.

  

War has many causes.

  

National security, ideology, terrorism, political beliefs, international conflicts, and genuine security concerns can all play a role.

  

The documentary’s argument is more subtle.

  

It asks us to consider whether **economic and political interests can influence decisions that are presented to the public mainly as matters of national security.**

  

**C1:**

And I think that’s why the documentary focuses so much on the Iraq War.

  

The public was told about serious security threats, but the documentary asks us to look at the larger political and economic environment surrounding the war.

  

**H:**

So the question isn’t:

  

“Was national security completely fake?”

  

It’s:

  

“Were there other interests influencing the decision as well?”

  

**E:**

Exactly.

  

That’s the critical-thinking point.

  

⸻

  

**4. Personal Experiences of War**

  

**H:**

Now I want to move away from politics for a moment.

  

We’ve talked about governments, companies, and money.

  

But what happens to ordinary people?

  

C1, you’ve prepared a personal perspective for us. Can you tell us about it?

  

**C1:**

Sure.

  

Imagine having a family member who goes to war.

  

At first, you might think about patriotism. You might think, “They’re protecting our country.”

  

But when the person doesn’t come home, the political discussion becomes very personal.

  

You don’t think about military budgets anymore.

  

You think about a person.

  

You think about their family, their memories, and the empty space they leave behind.

  

**H:**

That’s powerful.

  

And the documentary includes a similar personal story, right?

  

**C1:**

Yes.

  

The film introduces a Vietnam War veteran whose son was killed in the September 11 attacks.

  

After losing his son, he supported military action and wanted his son’s name placed on bombs used in Iraq.

  

I found this story especially complicated because it shows how personal grief can become connected to national decisions about war.

  

**E:**

And that’s an important contrast.

  

A government may discuss war using words like “strategy,” “security,” or “national interest.”

  

But for ordinary people, war can mean **loss, grief, injury, displacement, or fear.**

  

**C2:**

That’s why I think we shouldn’t only ask, “How much does a war cost the government?”

  

We should also ask:

  

**“How much does a war cost ordinary people?”**

  

⸻

  

**5. Economic Opportunity and Military Service**

  

**H:**

C2, you also wanted to talk about another personal story from the documentary.

  

**C2:**

Yes.

  

The documentary follows a young American who joins the military partly because of difficult economic circumstances.

  

That really made me think.

  

We often describe joining the military as a completely personal choice.

  

But how free is that choice if someone has very limited economic opportunities?

  

**H:**

So you’re saying economic inequality can influence who ends up fighting a war.

  

**C2:**

Yes.

  

If someone needs money, education, healthcare, or a stable career, the military can provide opportunities that they may not have elsewhere.

  

So the question becomes:

  

**Who fights the war, and who benefits from the economic system surrounding it?**

  

**E:**

That’s one of the most important questions in the documentary.

  

War doesn’t affect everyone equally.

  

Some people make political decisions about war.

  

Some companies produce weapons.

  

Some communities receive jobs.

  

And some people actually go to the battlefield.

  

**C1:**

And some families lose someone.

  

**H:**

So there can be a gap between the people who make decisions about war and the people who experience its consequences.

  

**E:**

Exactly.

  

⸻

  

**6. Is the Military-Industrial Complex Always Negative?**

  

**H:**

Let’s challenge the argument again.

  

Could someone say that the military-industrial complex is actually necessary?

  

After all, countries need weapons and military technology to protect themselves.

  

**E:**

Absolutely.

  

That’s an important counterargument.

  

A country cannot simply eliminate its military.

  

Defense industries can create technology, jobs, research, and national security capabilities.

  

The problem begins when **military power becomes an end in itself rather than a tool for security.**

  

**C2:**

So the real issue is balance?

  

**E:**

Yes.

  

We need to ask:

  

**How much military spending is actually necessary for security?**

  

And:

  

**At what point does spending become driven by political or economic interests rather than actual security needs?**

  

**C1:**

I think citizens also have a responsibility here.

  

We shouldn’t automatically accept the idea that more weapons always mean more safety.

  

**H:**

That’s an important point.

  

More military power does not automatically equal more security.

  

Sometimes security can also come from diplomacy, economic stability, education, international cooperation, and human rights.

  

⸻

  

**7. Connection to Sustainable Development Goals**

  

**H:**

Now let’s connect this documentary to our bigger topic: **Sustainable Development and Global Justice.**

  

Which Sustainable Development Goals are connected to the military-industrial complex?

  

**E:**

I would highlight at least three.

  

First, **SDG 16: Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions.**

  

If political decisions are strongly influenced by powerful economic interests, we need transparency and accountability.

  

Second, **SDG 8: Decent Work and Economic Growth.**

  

The documentary makes us think about how communities depend on defense-related jobs. We need economic opportunities that don’t make communities dependent on military spending.

  

Third, **SDG 10: Reduced Inequalities.**

  

As we discussed, the economic consequences of war are not equally distributed.

  

Some people have the power to make decisions, while others experience the consequences.

  

**C2:**

So sustainability isn’t only about the environment.

  

**E:**

Exactly.

  

Sustainability also means building societies where peace, equality, justice, and economic opportunity can continue in the long term.

  

**C1:**

And global justice means asking whether the costs and benefits of international decisions are fairly distributed.

  

⸻

  

**8. Final Discussion — Why Do We Fight?**

  

**H:**

We’ve talked about politics, economics, personal experiences, and sustainability.

  

So I want to end with the original question:

  

**Why do we fight?**

  

C1?

  

**C1:**

I think people fight for many reasons.

  

But after watching this documentary, I don’t think we should accept the official explanation without asking more questions.

  

We should always ask who makes the decision and who pays the price.

  

**H:**

C2?

  

**C2:**

For me, the biggest lesson is that war isn’t something that happens far away from ordinary life.

  

It can affect jobs, families, taxes, communities, and individual choices.

  

So we need to understand the system behind war, not just the war itself.

  

**H:**

And finally, our expert?

  

**E:**

I think the documentary doesn’t ask us to believe that every war is caused by profit.

  

It asks us to be more critical.

  

When military, political, and economic interests become connected, citizens need to ask difficult questions.

  

Who benefits?

  

Who decides?

  

Who fights?

  

And who pays the price?

  

**H:**

That’s a great way to end.
